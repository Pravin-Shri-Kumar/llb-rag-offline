INTRODUCTION

The President of India is the head of the Union and the first citizen of the country. Article 52 declares that there shall be a President of India, and Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in him. His name appears at the top of every great act of the State, from the appointment of ministers to the passing of laws and the grant of mercy. Yet he is a constitutional head, which means he holds these powers in his name but uses them on the advice of the government. He is best understood as the great seal of the nation, essential to every important act, yet guided by those who run the country.


ELECTION OF THE PRESIDENT

The President is not elected directly by the people. He is chosen by an electoral college. This college is made up of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States. The election is held by the system of proportional representation through a single transferable vote, and the voting is by secret ballot. The President holds office for a term of five years and is eligible for re-election.


POWERS AND FUNCTIONS OF THE PRESIDENT

The powers of the President may be arranged into six simple groups.

a) Executive Powers: All executive action of the Union is taken in the name of the President. He appoints the Prime Minister, the other ministers, the Governors of States, the judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts, the Attorney General, and the Comptroller and Auditor General.

b) Legislative Powers: He summons and ends the sessions of Parliament and may dissolve the Lok Sabha. Every bill passed by Parliament becomes law only after he gives his assent. When Parliament is not in session, he may issue an ordinance under Article 123, which has the same force as a law.

c) Financial Powers: A money bill can be introduced in Parliament only on his recommendation, and he causes the annual budget to be laid before Parliament.

d) Judicial Powers: Under Article 72, he has the power to grant pardons, and to suspend, remit, or reduce the punishment of a person who has been convicted.

e) Diplomatic and Military Powers: He represents India in its dealings with other nations, and treaties are made in his name. He is also the supreme commander of the defence forces of the country.

f) Emergency Powers: He may proclaim a National Emergency, a State Emergency, or a Financial Emergency to protect the nation in times of grave crisis.


POSITION OF THE PRESIDENT

Although the powers listed above are wide, the President is a constitutional head and not a ruler. Article 74 provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers, headed by the Prime Minister, to aid and advise the President, and that he shall act in accordance with such advice. He may send the advice back once for reconsideration, but if the same advice is returned to him, he must accept it. This shows that the real power of the Union lies with the Council of Ministers, while the President gives the acts of the government their constitutional form.


LANDMARK CASE

In Shamsher Singh versus State of Punjab (1974), the Supreme Court held that the President is a constitutional head who exercises his powers on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, and not according to his own personal will. This case confirms the true position of the President in the Indian system.


CONCLUSION

The President of India holds a high and dignified office. He is elected by a special electoral college, and in his name rest the executive, legislative, financial, judicial, diplomatic, military, and emergency powers of the Union. Yet he remains a constitutional head who acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. He is the great seal of the nation, powerful in name and essential to every important act of the State, but guided by the government that the people have chosen.
